# The story

The town's story, as the owner wrote it (Nov 25). It is backstory only: the game plays as before, and the story comes
out in what people say. The regulars' own lines are in `js/words.js` ("Town: the story", said after their three everyday
lines, `townRegLine` in town.js); what each townsperson carries of it is `LIFE_STORY` in `js/lives.js`, laid over their
mind so the built-in mind and a talk model both have it. New lines must agree with the text below and stay as sidelong
as it is: nobody explains what happened to Lucia.

Who is who in the game: Ruth (bakery), Marisol (coffee), Dot (diner), Hank (hardware), Earl (gun shop) and Gus (bank)
are `TOWN_REGS`; Pearl (`f80_jacket`, shares Hank's house), Cal (`cal_m`, bait and boat rental), Brianna (`f16_teen`),
her grandmother Denise (`f50_scrubs`) and Jayden (`m25_hoodie`, the ferry dock) are library people. Lucia is nobody you
meet.

You got off the last ferry a little after eleven. The lot was mostly empty except for Gus’s car, parked crooked under the light with the driver’s window all the way down. The door sat open a couple of inches. You walked over.

The seat was pushed back. Keys lay on the passenger side. A paper cup from Dot’s rested on the floorboard, a little coffee still in it, lid off, a wet ring on the mat. His jacket was bunched on the back seat. The radio played low, a Mobile station fading in and out of static. You smelled the coffee, the old mildew in the carpet, and cigarette smoke that wasn’t his. The edge of the seat was damp where mist had come through the open window. You left the door the way you found it.

On Bayou Lane the porch light was the only one still on. Brianna sat with her knees up, hoodie sleeves pulled over her hands, phone face-down on the step beside her. She tracked you the whole way past the house and only looked away once you were beyond the mailbox.

You slept a few hours in the room over the old insurance office. In the morning the ferry lot was empty. The space where Gus’s car had been was just dry pavement.

Marisol already had the coffee on. She poured a cup and stayed behind the counter.

“They moved it sometime after four,” she said. “Jayden saw lights. Didn’t see who drove.” She wiped her hands on a towel that didn’t need it. “Gus called me at midnight. Said he wanted to talk about my mother. I went to the lot. Waited in the car. He never came. Around three I saw a boat heading north with no lights and I left.” She looked at the door. “I told the deputy. He wrote it down.”

You drank. She didn’t ask what else had been in the car.

“Ruth called this morning,” she said after a minute. “Asked if I’d talked to him. I said no. She hung up.”

Outside, Hank’s truck sat in front of the hardware store with the door open. He stood beside it, looking at the seat, and shut the door when he saw you.

“Pearl wants to know if you’re coming by,” he said. “She’s got the scrapbook out. Says a page is missing.” He waited. You didn’t answer. He nodded and went inside, leaving the truck unlocked.

Pearl had the book open on the kitchen table. The place where the page had been was a clean cut. She set a jar of preserves next to it.

“Lucia used to buy one every spring,” she said. “Exact change. I started keeping the book that year.” She didn’t touch the empty spot. “Marisol sat with it at my birthday. Left quick. I didn’t say anything.” She looked toward the window. “Hank was on the water that night. He doesn’t talk about it. I stopped asking a long time ago.”

You left the jar where it was.

Ruth was on her porch with a cup she wasn’t drinking. She watched you come up the steps.

“Denise said there was a woman in his car,” she said. “Wasn’t me.” She turned the cup. “Hank tells people I won’t speak to Pearl. I speak to her. I just don’t sit in that kitchen.” She glanced down the street. Earl’s truck was parked in front of the gun shop. “Gus lived four doors down all these years. Never asked me what I heard. Not once.”

She stood and picked up the broom.

“People stayed quiet about that girl longer than made any sense,” she said. She swept a small pile of leaves off the porch and let them drop. Neither of you said Lucia’s name.

Down at the rental dock Cal had the log book open. He didn’t look up right away.

“Boat Seven went out last night,” he said. “Line was coiled neat. Not many left who do it that way.” He closed the book. “Earl still comes by the ramp sometimes after midnight. Doesn’t ride. Just watches.”

You stood there while a charter boat idled past. Across the water the marsh was bright and empty. Somewhere north a small outboard moved steady and low, already far enough out that the sound barely reached.
