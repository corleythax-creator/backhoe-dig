Prologue

What Pearl Remembers

From the scrapbook of Mrs. Pearl Dubois, Live Oak Drive. The pages after August 1987 were left blank for thirty-nine years. These were written in them in November.

I have kept this book since 1966, the year Hank and I came to Live Oak Drive. I glue in what happens. Church suppers, ribbons, weddings, the year the ferry ramp washed out, the year the shrimp didn't come. I never wrote anything of my own in it, because I always thought a scrapbook ought to be what the town said and not what I said. I have changed my mind about that. The town has been saying the wrong thing for a long time.

So here is what I know, and who everybody is, the way I'd tell it to a stranger at my kitchen table. You came to town in October with a yellow machine on a trailer. I saw you go by. You waved at the house, which is more than most do. This is for you, I suppose, though I didn't know it yet.

* * *

I will start with Hank, because I always do.

Hank Dubois was twenty-two when I married him and he smelled of diesel and bait and he still does, a little, though he has sold hammers for twenty-five years. He shrimped out of this harbor on his father's boat and then on his own, until the boats cost more than the shrimp paid. He is a good man and a quiet one, and he has two silences. One of them means he is tired. The other one means he knows something and has decided he oughtn't. I have been married to the second silence since the summer of 1987, and I am tired of it.

Hank learned his knots from Earl Pruitt. Every boy on this water did, in those days. Earl would sit on the ferry rail between crossings and show you a bowline one-handed, with a cigarette in the other, and if you got it wrong he'd untie it and hand it back without a word until you got it right. Then he'd show you how to coil a line flat on the dock so it looked like a rose, and tuck the end under. Nobody needs to do that. Earl said a man who leaves a line in a heap leaves everything in a heap.

Earl ran that ferry from 1966. Not one missed crossing in twenty years. The company gave him a gold watch with his initials on the back, and we had a supper in the church hall, and Walt Ames said the blessing and Ruth Halvorsen made the cake. I have a photograph of Earl holding the watch up to the camera like it was a fish he'd caught. I glued it on the page facing the ferry ramp clipping. He looked happier in that picture than I ever saw him look again.

The next fall he quit the boat. Said it was his knees. I watched Earl Pruitt climb the church steps two at a time that Christmas.

* * *

Ruth Halvorsen and I have known each other sixty years and been friends for maybe forty of them. She taught third grade on Pine Court for longer than some of my grandchildren have been alive, and if you were born in this town between 1965 and 2005 you learned your letters from Ruth or you were sent to Catholic school. She never married. She took care of her mother until her mother was ninety-four. When she retired she bought the bakery, and now she gets up at four instead of five and seems happier for it.

Ruth is the kindest woman I know and the most frightened, and it took me a long time to see those were the same thing. Something happened to her in 1987. She stopped coming to my kitchen. She came back, a year or so later, but she never again sat with her back to the window.

She lives four doors from Gus Lindqvist. I have never once seen her look at his house.

* * *

Gus came to the bank that same summer, twenty-five years old, from Hattiesburg, in a brown suit that was too warm for August. He was a nervous young man who said thank you twice for everything. By that winter he had stopped saying it at all. He was made manager in 2004. He has eaten lunch at Dot's counter at a quarter past twelve every weekday for as long as anyone can remember, and he leaves a dollar on the counter, folded once, and Dot puts it in a jar she calls the Gus Fund and has never told me what she means to do with it.

Gus does not fish. Gus does not go on boats. He came on the ferry exactly once that I know of, the summer he arrived, and was sick over the rail in front of Earl, who held his collar so he wouldn't go over and never mentioned it to a soul. I know because Earl told Hank and Hank told me, which is how I know most things.

* * *

Dot Kowalski paints, or she did. Most of the marsh pictures on the diner walls are hers, with prices on little cards that nobody pays, because they are too sad to hang in a house. She bought the diner twenty-six years ago when her husband died and she needed somewhere to be at five in the morning. She knows what everyone orders, and she knows when they stop ordering it, and she will tell you which is the more interesting.

Walt Ames has been the deacon since before the bell went slow. He drives the old ones to the doctor, me included, and unlocks the hall, and sits with the dying. He has heard more confessions than the priest across the water and he never repeats a word, which is a fine quality in a deacon and a maddening one in a neighbor.

Owen Brandt's daddy fished a flat-bottomed skiff out of the north marsh his whole life and never once got lost, even in fog. Owen grew up in that boat and then spent fifteen years as a game warden in the same channels, writing tickets to boys he went to school with. Three years ago he quit and opened a brewery in the old ice house. He says beer is easier to make than enemies. I have noticed he still knows which way the tide is running without looking.

Cal Mercer was one of Hank's boys on the docks. He went into the Navy, came home, and couldn't sleep more than a mile from salt water, so he rents boats now, and Callie runs the toy store across the street, and their boy Cody ties knots like a man of sixty. Cal coils his lines the way Earl taught Hank to teach him. I can see the rental slips from the church parking lot. I would know Cal's coil anywhere.

* * *

The young ones I know less well, which is a shame on me.

Denise works nights at the hospital in Pascagoula and raises her girl Brianna on her own. Brianna is sixteen and has her mother's eyes and her own opinions, and she sits on their porch on Bayou Lane half the night with her phone and sees everything that moves on that street. I would have been the same at her age, if there had been anything to see.

Nathan at the bank is a nice boy from Pascagoula who has lived here six years, and nobody has had him to supper, which I mean to fix. Travis crews the charter boats and is on the water before the gulls. Jayden works the ferry ramp, and he is saving for a boat, and Hank says he has bought a lot of new things lately for a boy who ties up ferries for a living. Hank does not say things like that unless he means them.

And there is the girl at the coffee shop. Marisol. She came in the spring from Mobile, and she is pretty and clever and she makes a good cup of coffee, which nobody in this town managed before. She came to my birthday party in April, because the whole town did, and she sat on my settee and looked at my scrapbook while I was in the kitchen, and was gone by the time I came back with the tea.

I didn't think anything about it at the time. It was a full house.

* * *

Now I will tell you about the girl from the bank, because nobody else will, and because I am eighty and tired of being polite.

She was twenty-three. She had a baby at home and no husband, which in 1987 people still talked about. She worked the teller window at First Lake Bank and she came to the church sale every spring and bought two jars of my pear preserves and paid with exact change, every time, counted out in her palm. She had learned to read in Ruth Halvorsen's class. She told me so once, and said Miss Halvorsen was the reason she could do numbers in her head.

In August she was gone. The bank was short a great deal of money. The paper put those two facts next to each other and let people draw the line themselves, and people did, because it was easier than the other lines they could have drawn.

I did not draw it. I glued that clipping in this book so I would always remember that I didn't.

That page isn't here anymore.

I have not said her name aloud in thirty-nine years, and neither has anybody else in this town. I am going to leave it off this page too, for one more chapter. You'll hear it soon enough. When you dug that box out of the clay on the island, every person I just told you about heard it, in their own head, all at once.

Hank came home that night and sat in his chair without turning on the television. That was the second silence. I sat across from him and waited, the way I have been waiting since 1987.

He didn't say anything. But at two in the morning I heard the truck start.

Chapter One

Boat Seven

The bucket teeth hit metal at a quarter to six, just as the last light went off the water.

You felt it through the seat before you heard it, a dull clank that wasn't rock. You set the bucket down, climbed out and knelt in the cut. Four feet down in the grey clay was a steel box about the size of a bread loaf. Its handle had rusted to lace. Through the scale on the lid you could just read the stamp:

FIRST LAKE BANK · 1987

It was too heavy for what it was. When you shook it, something inside slid and settled like a deck of cards.

You took it across on the last ferry. Jayden was working the ramp, hood up against the wind. He looked at the box under your arm a beat too long and said nothing, which wasn't like Jayden.

* * *

At 5:10 the next morning, Cal Mercer walked down to the rental slips behind Tackle & Trail with his coffee and counted his boats the way he'd done every morning for thirty years. He got to six and stopped.

Boat Seven was gone. The line hadn't been cut. Someone had untied it neatly and coiled it on the dock the way Cal himself would.

Travis spotted it half an hour later from the charter boat, drifting off the north marsh with the engine tilted up. It was empty except for a man's wristwatch on the bench seat, still ticking, and a First Lake Bank deposit slip folded into a square. Someone had written one line on the back in pencil:

She knows about the box.

* * *

By 9:20 there was a small crowd outside the bank. Nathan, the loan officer, stood on the steps with his keys out and his face pale. He didn't have the vault code. Only Gus did.

“He's never late,” Nathan said, to nobody. “He's never anything.”

Across the street, Marisol Vega had stopped wiping the window of Mountain Coffee. Dot Kowalski came out of the Main St. Diner in her apron and stood with her arms folded. Up the block, Earl Pruitt unlocked Lakeside Guns as if it were any other morning. He didn't look over once.

Ruth Halvorsen brought a tray of day-old rolls from the bakery for the people waiting, because that's what Ruth did. When she reached you, she glanced at the bulge in your jacket where the strongbox sat wrapped in a shop rag.

“You found something on the island,” she said quietly. It wasn't a question. “Don't open it in town, dear. Not here.”

Then she moved on down the line, smiling and handing out rolls.

* * *

Denise came home from the night shift in Pascagoula at 7:40, still in her scrubs, and heard the news from Brianna at the kitchen table. She sat down without taking her shoes off.

“I passed his car,” she said. “Gus's. At the ferry lot, about three this morning. The dome light was on.” She rubbed her eyes. “There was somebody sitting in it. I figured it was him.”

“Was it?”

Denise was quiet for a long moment.

“It was a woman,” she said.

Chapter Two

The Coil

Cal Mercer had the rope laid out on the tailgate of his truck when you found him, the way a doctor lays out an X-ray.

“Look at that,” he said. “Go on. Look at it.”

It was Boat Seven's bow line, twenty feet of three-strand nylon, gone green at the eye. Whoever untied it had coiled it clockwise into a flat spiral, each turn tight against the last, the tail tucked under so the whole thing lay flat on the planks like a dinner plate.

“Flemish coil,” Cal said. “Nobody does that anymore. Takes a minute and a half and it don't do a thing but look nice. Weekend people throw a line in a heap. Kids throw it in the water.” He touched the tucked tail with one finger. “And there's only three men I ever saw tuck the end like that.”

“Who?”

“Me.” He held up one finger. “Hank Dubois, who taught me when I was eleven years old and dumber than that piling.” Second finger. He looked out at the water a long time before the third came up. “And Earl Pruitt. Who taught Hank.”

A pelican dropped into the channel behind him and came up with nothing.

“I was home asleep,” Cal said, before you could ask. “Callie'll tell you. She'll tell you I snore, too.”

* * *

Lakeside Guns smelled of gun oil and burnt coffee. Earl Pruitt was behind the counter with a lever-action broken down on a towel in front of him, working a brass rod through the barrel with slow, even strokes. He didn't look up when the bell over the door rang.

You set the watch on the glass.

The rod stopped. Just for a second, the length of one breath, and then it went on again, in and out.

“Where'd you get that.”

“Boat Seven. This morning. Travis found it on the seat.”

Earl set the rod down. He picked the watch up by its band, as if it were hot, and turned it over to the engraving. E.P. 1986. His thumb moved across the letters once.

“The ferry company gave me this,” he said. “Twenty years without a missed crossing. They had a supper for it at the church hall. Walt said the blessing.” He put it back on the glass, face down. “I lost it in the water off the north marsh in the fall of '87. Went over the rail reaching for a line. Never saw it again.”

“It's still running.”

“Then somebody wound it.” He picked the brass rod back up. “Which means somebody's had it thirty-nine years and kept it wound. You go figure out who that is, and you come tell me, because I'd like to know too.”

You were at the door when he spoke again, still not looking up.

“That box you dug up. You still got it?”

You didn't answer.

“Good,” Earl said. “Keep not saying.”

* * *

The locksmith from the mainland couldn't come until Thursday. Nathan told you that on the bank steps at closing time, his tie loosened, his keys still in his hand as if he'd forgotten he was holding them.

“The vault's on a time lock and a combination,” he said. “Gus changed the combination every quarter and he never wrote it down. Never. He used to say a number on paper is a number in somebody else's pocket.” He laughed, a short, unhappy sound. “Corporate wants a report. What am I supposed to tell them? Our manager went fishing in a rental boat at three in the morning and forgot to come back?”

“Did Gus fish?”

Nathan thought about it.

“No,” he said slowly. “No. He got seasick on the ferry.”

Chapter Three

The Missing Page

Hometown Hardware sold carpenter's pencils from a coffee can by the register, flat red ones with the store's name stamped on the side in gold that rubbed off after a week. Hank Dubois kept the can filled himself. He also kept a spiral notebook under the counter where he wrote down every sale over five dollars and most of the ones under, in a hand so square it looked printed.

“Pencils,” Hank said, running his thumb down the column. “Wes bought two in September. The Park girl bought one for her clipboard. Bobby bought a box of twelve for the lawn crew. That's it since summer.” He closed the notebook. “And I keep one in my shirt pocket and so does every man who ever worked a boat out of this town, because a pen won't write wet.”

His hand went to his shirt pocket as he said it. The pocket was empty.

He saw you notice. He didn't explain.

* * *

Mrs. Pearl Dubois received you in the front room on Live Oak Drive with iced tea in glasses so thin you were afraid to set yours down. She was eighty and sat as straight as a girl at a piano lesson. The scrapbook was already on the coffee table between you, as if she'd been expecting someone to ask.

“Hank says I shouldn't talk to you,” she said. “Hank has said that about one thing or another for fifty-six years, and I have mostly ignored him, and we have done just fine.”

She opened the book to August 1987. Church suppers. A blue ribbon for pear preserves. A clipping about the new ferry ramp, with Earl Pruitt squinting at the camera in his captain's hat. And then a stub: a page cut out so close to the binding that only a quarter inch of paper was left, sliced clean with a razor.

“I didn't do that,” Pearl said. “I want you to know that first. Somebody sat in this room, at this table, while I was in the kitchen making tea, and cut it out of my book. I didn't notice for a month.”

“When?”

“Last spring.”

“Who was here last spring?”

She smiled at you as if you were a slow but promising student.

“Everyone, dear. It was my eightieth birthday. Half the town came through that door.” She turned the stub over with one fingernail. “But I remember what was on the page. I've a good memory for the things I glue down. It was the Mississippi Press, the twenty-second of August. A teller from First Lake Bank, a young woman, had gone missing. The paper said the bank was short two hundred and eleven thousand dollars, and the paper said it like the two things went together.”

“What was her name?”

“Lucia. Lucia Vega.” Pearl folded her hands. “She used to buy my preserves at the church sale and she always paid with exact change. I never believed a word of that article. Never. I kept it because I wanted to remember that I didn't believe it.”

The ice settled in your glass.

Vega.

“Mrs. Dubois,” you said, “does Hank know what happened to her?”

Pearl looked at the window, at the street, where Hank's truck was just now turning into the drive.

“Hank was on the water that night,” she said quietly. “Everybody who mattered was on the water that night. Earl, and Hank, and that skiff of Owen's daddy's. I was home with the radio. Ruth was home with her lesson plans.” She closed the book. “Ask Ruth what she was grading at two in the morning. I always wondered.”

Chapter Four

Exact Change

Mountain Coffee closed at four. At five past, Marisol Vega was alone behind the counter, stacking cups, and she didn't turn around when you came in.

“We're closed.”

“Lucia Vega,” you said.

The cup in her hand stopped halfway to the stack. She set it down carefully, the way Earl had set down the watch, and for a moment the only sound was the drip of the espresso machine cooling.

“She was my mother,” Marisol said. “I was a baby. My aunt raised me in Mobile and told me my mother ran off with a man and a bag of money, and I believed it until last year, when my aunt was dying and told me she never believed it either.” She turned around. Her eyes were dry and very tired. “So, yes. I came here to ask about 1987. And everybody here is so nice to me. They tip well. They ask about my day. And not one of them will tell me anything.”

“You were in Gus's car.”

She didn't deny it.

“He called me at midnight,” she said. “I didn't even know he had my number. He said, ‘Miss Vega, I've been a coward for thirty-nine years and I'm too old to keep at it. Meet me at the ferry lot at two and I'll tell you what happened to your mother.’ He said someone had dug something up on the island and there wasn't any more time.”

She meant you. You both knew it.

“I got there at quarter to two. His car was there, unlocked, keys on the seat. He wasn't. I sat in it because it was cold and because I thought he'd come back.” She wrapped her arms around herself. “Around three I saw a light on the water. A little boat, going north toward the marsh with no running lights, just a flashlight. Then the flashlight went off. I sat there until half past three and then I drove home and I didn't sleep.”

“Why didn't you tell anyone?”

Marisol laughed. It wasn't a good sound.

“The last time a Vega was near a lot of trouble in this town,” she said, “they wrote it in the paper as if she'd caused it.”

* * *

She walked you to the door and unlocked it. Then, with her hand still on the bolt, she said one more thing.

“He wrote something down. On the phone. I could hear the pencil. When he said ‘someone dug something up,’ he stopped and I heard him write, and then he said, ‘She knows about the box. I've just put it in writing so I don't lose my nerve.’”

You thought of the deposit slip folded into a square on the seat of Boat Seven.

“Who's ‘she’?”

“I thought he meant me,” Marisol said. “I don't anymore.”

Chapter Five

Lesson Plans

Ruth Halvorsen's porch light was on, and Ruth was sitting under it in a cardigan with a plate of shortbread on her knees, as if she had known exactly when you'd come up the walk. Maybe she had. It was a small town and Ruth had been watching it for a long time.

“Sit down,” she said. “Have one. They're better than the rolls.”

You sat. You didn't take one.

“Pearl says to ask what you were grading at two in the morning on the twenty-first of August, 1987.”

Ruth smiled down at the shortbread.

“Pearl has been wanting to ask me that herself for thirty-nine years,” she said. “She's too polite. That's the whole trouble with this town, you know. Everybody is too polite to ask and too ashamed to tell, and in between the two a young woman just disappears.”

A truck went by slowly on Magnolia Lane, then another. Ruth watched them both until their taillights turned the corner.

“I wasn't grading anything,” she said. “I was at the bank. Lucia had a key and she'd asked me to come, because she'd found something in the vault that she couldn't take to Gus, and she trusted me because I'd taught her to read.” Ruth's hands were very still on the plate. “She'd found the box. A box that wasn't on any ledger. Somebody had been running money through it, other people's money, for years. She wanted a witness before she took it to the state.”

“What was in it?”

“Cards,” said Ruth. “Index cards. A name on each one and a number. Who'd paid, who'd been paid. Half this town's grandfathers were in that box, and a few of the fathers.” She looked at you directly for the first time. “And it was sitting in your backhoe's cab right now, I expect, if you were half as careful as Earl told you to be.”

You hadn't told anyone where it was.

“What happened to Lucia?”

“We were in the vault when the front door opened,” Ruth said. “I hid. God forgive me, I hid behind the safe-deposit wall like a child, and Lucia went out to see who it was, and I heard voices, two men's, and then her voice, and then the door. I waited an hour. When I came out the box was gone and so was she.” Her voice didn't break. You had the sense that it had broken so many times over the years that there was nothing left in it to break. “I never said. Not to the police, not to the paper, not to Walt in his church. I had a job and a mother to look after and I was afraid. I've been afraid every day since.”

“Whose voices?”

Ruth set the plate down on the porch boards.

“One of them was Gus,” she said. “He was twenty-five and he'd been at the bank a month. He sounded scared to death.” She stood, slowly, one hand on the porch rail. “The other one I'd known all my life. Go home, dear. Lock your door. I've told you more tonight than I've told anyone, and I am too old to be brave twice in one week.”

She went inside. The porch light went off.

* * *

You took the last ferry back to the island at eleven. Jayden wasn't on the ramp. A boy you didn't know waved you on.

Halfway across, you saw the light.

It was on Corley's Island, on the mesa, right where your cut was. A single flashlight beam, low to the ground, sweeping back and forth across the dug clay. Then a second beam joined it. And then, carrying across the dark water so clearly it might have been next to you, came a sound you knew better than your own voice.

Your backhoe's engine, turning over.
